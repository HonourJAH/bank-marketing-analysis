# What the data says about who buys a term deposit

## About this report

A Portuguese bank contacted customers by phone between May 2008 and November 2010 to sell a term deposit, a savings product in which money is held for a fixed period. This report examines who subscribed and what was associated with a successful campaign.

**Data and definitions**

- The analysis covers 41,176 contact records. Twelve exact duplicates were removed from the original 41,188.
- The data has no customer ID, so one person may appear more than once. Each record is referred to as a "customer" for readability.
- **Yes rate:** the share of customers contacted who subscribed. The overall yes rate is 11.3%, and every group in this report is compared with it.
- The findings describe patterns in the data. They do not establish causes.
- Percentages are rounded, so a column may not add to exactly 100%.

## Question 1. How many customers subscribed, and how many calls did it take?

**Findings**

*Table 1.1. Calls and subscriptions by outcome*

| Outcome | Customers | Share of customers | Calls made | Share of calls | Average calls per customer | Median calls per customer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subscribed (yes) | 4,639 | 11.3% | 9,519 | 9.0% | 2.05 | 2 |
| Did not subscribe (no) | 36,537 | 88.7% | 96,216 | 91.0% | 2.63 | 2 |
| **Total** | **41,176** | **100%** | **105,735** | **100%** | **2.57** | **2** |

*Table 1.2. Calls per subscription*

| Campaign measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Total calls made | 105,735 |
| Customers who subscribed | 4,639 |
| Calls per subscription | 22.8 |

- 4,639 of 41,176 customers subscribed, a yes rate of 11.3%.
- The typical customer received two calls whether or not they subscribed: the median is 2 in both groups. The average is 2.05 calls for customers who subscribed and 2.63 for those who did not (Table 1.1).
- Customers who did not subscribe were 88.7% of the call list but received 91.0% of all calls.
- The campaign made 22.8 calls for every subscription (Table 1.2).

**What this means**

Calling effort was spread fairly evenly across customers, and most received about two calls. The higher average among customers who did not subscribe, combined with identical medians, suggests that a small group received many calls and mostly did not subscribe. Question 6 examines this.

**Limitations**

- The 22.8 calls per subscription is a campaign-wide ratio that includes every call made to customers who never subscribed. It is not the cost of winning one customer, because the data contains no call costs or deposit values.
- The data cannot show whether additional calls reduce the likelihood of a yes. Customers who subscribe probably receive no further calls, so the two groups are not directly comparable.
- Only calls made during this campaign are counted.

- The yes rate is highest at the two ends of the age range. Customers aged 65 and over subscribed at 47.3%, and customers under 25 at 24.0%, against 11.3% overall (Table 2.1).
- The high rate among older customers is consistent. Every five-year group from 65 upward has a yes rate between 40.7% and 52.9% (Table 2.2), so the result does not depend on one narrow age range.
- Among younger customers the signal comes from ages 20 to 24, with a yes rate of 22.4% (222 of 992). Ages 15 to 19 show 45.3%, but there are only 75 customers in that group, too few to draw a conclusion.
- Customers aged 35 to 54 are the largest part of the call list and responded below average. Their combined yes rate is 8.7% (Table 2.3).
- The yes rate changes sharply at age 60. It is 10.7% for ages 55 to 59 and 30.0% for ages 60 to 64 (Table 2.2). The number of customers also falls, from 3,036 to 530.
- Customers under 25 and 65 and over together are 4.2% of the call list but account for 12.3% of all subscriptions. Customers aged 35 to 54 are 53.9% of the list and account for 41.4% of subscriptions (Table 2.3).

**What this means**

The age groups that respond best, customers aged 60 and over and customers in their early twenties, are a small part of the call list. Most calls went to customers aged 35 to 54, who responded below average. Whether shifting calls toward the better-responding ages would pay off cannot be determined from this data alone.

**Limitations**

- The data shows an association with age, not the reason for it. Question 3 shows that retirement does not explain the change at age 60, because the same jump appears among customers who are not retired.
- Age may overlap with other factors. Question 3 tests job, and the time of year customers were contacted is tested later.
- The age groups were chosen for this analysis. A wide group can hide a change within it: the original 55 to 64 group concealed the jump at age 60.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.

*How the expected yes rate is calculated.* Age affects the yes rate, so a group with many older customers would look strong for that reason alone. For each group, we take the yes rate of customers in all other groups within each age group, and apply those rates to the group's own mix of ages. The result is the yes rate the group would have if it behaved like everyone else of the same age. The Result column compares actual and expected rates, allowing for the variation that chance alone produces in a group of that size. "Borderline" means the gap is only just larger than chance would produce.

*By job*

- Students (31.4%) and retired customers (25.3%) have the highest yes rates, against 11.3% overall (Table 3.1).
- Blue-collar customers have the lowest yes rate (6.9%), followed by services (8.1%) and entrepreneurs (8.5%). Blue-collar customers are 22.5% of the call list but 13.8% of subscriptions.
- Admin. customers are the largest source of subscriptions (29.1%), with a yes rate of 13.0%.
- Most other jobs are close to the overall rate, between 10.0% and 11.2% (Table 3.1).

*By education*

- University graduates have the highest yes rate among the large groups, at 13.7%. They are 29.5% of the call list but 36.0% of subscriptions (Table 3.2).
- The three groups with basic schooling respond below average: 10.2% for 4 years, 8.2% for 6 years and 7.8% for 9 years.
- Customers with unknown education have a yes rate of 14.5% (1,730 customers).
- Only 18 customers are recorded as illiterate, too few to interpret.

*Does age explain the job results?*

- The high yes rate among retired customers reflects age, not retirement. Among customers aged 65 and over, retired customers subscribed at 47.2% and customers in all other jobs at 47.6% (Table 3.4). Retired customers aged 45 to 54 subscribed at 7.1%, below the 8.7% of other jobs.
- The jump at age 60 appears with or without retirement. The yes rate rises from 12.3% to 31.1% among retired customers and from 10.3% to 29.2% among those not retired (Table 3.5).
- Retired customers aged 55 to 64 looked better than others in that range (17.1% against 12.4%, Table 3.4) because more of them are at the upper end: 225 of the 877 retired customers in that range are aged 60 to 64, against 305 of the 2,689 other customers (Tables 3.3 and 3.5).
- Student status carries its own signal. Students under 25 subscribed at 37.8% against 16.4% for other jobs, and students aged 25 to 34 at 27.9% against 11.7% (Table 3.4).

*Do the job and education results hold once age is allowed for?*

- Students respond at more than twice the rate expected for their ages (31.4% against 14.0%). Unemployed customers (14.2% against 10.4%) and admin. customers (13.0% against 9.9%) are also higher than expected (Table 3.6).
- Blue-collar customers (6.9% against 11.2%) and services customers (8.1% against 11.1%) are lower than expected, so age does not explain their low yes rates (Table 3.6).
- Retired customers are in line with the expected rate (25.3% against 24.7%), as are management, technician, self-employed, housemaid and unknown (Table 3.6).
- University graduates are higher than expected (13.7% against 9.7%). All three basic-schooling groups are lower than expected (Table 3.7).
- Age hid part of the education pattern. Customers with 4 years of basic schooling looked close to average (10.2%, Table 3.2) but are well below the 13.1% expected for their ages (Table 3.7).
- Customers with unknown education respond modestly above expectation (14.5% against 12.5%).

**What this means**

Student status, an admin. job and a university degree are associated with a higher yes rate than age alone would predict, while blue-collar work, services work and basic schooling are associated with a lower one. These are candidates for prioritising or deprioritising when deciding whom to call, but the data cannot show whether calling more customers in the higher-responding groups would produce more subscriptions. Retirement does not need to be treated as a separate signal. Age is the signal: customers aged 60 and over respond well whether or not they are retired.

**Limitations**

- The data shows associations, not reasons. It cannot explain why any group responds differently.
- The expected yes rates allow for age only. They do not allow for the month of contact, the contact method or the economic climate, which later questions examine.
- Job and education may overlap, for example blue-collar work and basic schooling. They were not tested together.
- Many groups were tested, so one or two could differ from expectation by chance alone. The borderline results (entrepreneur and high school) should not be relied on.
- The expected rate for retired customers rests on only 103 customers aged 65 and over in other jobs (Table 3.3), so it is the least solid comparison.
- Jobs and education levels are as recorded by the bank, and the data cannot show how accurate or current they are. Customers with an unknown job or education are kept as their own group.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.

*Housing and personal loans*

- Having a loan makes little difference to the yes rate. Customers with a housing loan subscribed at 11.6% and those without at 10.9%. Customers with a personal loan subscribed at 10.9% and those without at 11.3%, against 11.3% overall (Table 4.1).
- The 990 customers whose loan status is unknown are the same customers in both columns. Their yes rate is 10.8%, close to the overall rate.
- Allowing for age does not change this. Every loan group is within 0.7 points of its expected rate, and the differences are too small to matter for deciding whom to call (Table 4.2).

*Credit default*

- Only 3 customers are recorded as having credit in default, too few to interpret (Table 4.1).
- Customers whose credit default status is unknown subscribed at 5.2%, less than half the overall 11.3%. Customers with a recorded "no" subscribed at 12.9%. Unknown customers are 20.9% of the call list but 9.5% of subscriptions (Table 4.1).
- The weak response is not explained by age. The unknown group's actual yes rate of 5.2% is far below the 11.9% expected for its ages (Table 4.2).

*Contact method and month*

- Customers with an unknown credit default status were contacted differently. 49.2% were reached by telephone, against 33.2% of customers with a recorded status. Telephone has the lower yes rate in both groups (Table 4.3).
- Unknown customers were concentrated in May (39.6% of the group against 31.8% of recorded customers), the month with the lowest yes rate among recorded customers (7.2%). They were almost absent from March, September, October and December (6 to 18 customers a month), the months in which recorded customers responded best, at 43.5% to 50.8% (Table 4.4).
- Unknown customers respond less within every group tested. By cellular they subscribed at 6.7% against 16.4%, and by telephone at 3.6% against 5.9% (Table 4.3). In every month with at least 100 unknown customers, their yes rate is below that of recorded customers (Table 4.4).
- Contact type and month together explain a little over half of the difference. Recorded customers subscribed at 12.9% against 5.2% for unknown customers, a gap of 7.7 points. Allowing for both contact type and month, the expected rate for unknown customers is 8.6%, leaving a gap of 3.4 points (Tables 4.3 and 4.5).

**What this means**

Whether a customer has a housing or personal loan tells the bank very little about who will subscribe. The useful signal is a missing credit default record. These customers are one in five on the call list and respond at about half the rate of other customers. Part of the gap comes from how and when they were called, mostly by telephone and in May, and part is unexplained. They are a candidate for lower priority, but the data cannot show whether the missing record itself matters or whether it goes together with something else that does.

**Limitations**

- The data shows associations, not reasons. "Unknown" means only that the bank has no record, and the data does not say why.
- Age, contact type and month were each tested separately, not all together. The combined comparison of contact type and month relies on small groups, so it is the least solid figure here.
- Housing loan and personal loan were not tested together, because each is within one point of the overall yes rate.
- Whether a customer has a loan is as recorded at the time of the call. The data does not show when it was taken or how large it is.
- Fewer than 100 unknown customers were contacted in March, September, October and December, so no yes rate is shown for them.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.

*Contact type*

- Customers contacted on a cellular phone subscribed at 14.7% and those contacted by telephone at 5.2%, against 11.3% overall. Telephone is 36.5% of the call list but 17.0% of subscriptions (Table 5.1).
- The difference between the two is concentrated in May, June and July. Those three months account for 13,825 of the 15,041 telephone customers (8,250 + 4,498 + 1,077, Table 5.4), and telephone customers there subscribed at 3.3%, 4.7% and 5.4%, against 11.1%, 42.6% and 9.7% for cellular.
- In April, August, October and November the two channels are close, and the gaps run in both directions: telephone is higher in April (24.6% against 20.2%), August (13.4% against 10.5%) and November (11.8% against 10.0%), and lower in October (40.3% against 44.9%). The telephone groups in those months are small, between 154 and 425 customers (Table 5.4).

*Month*

- Four months have far higher yes rates than the rest: March (50.5%), September (44.9%), October (43.9%) and December (48.9%). Together they are 4.9% of the call list but account for 20.2% of all subscriptions, with a combined yes rate of 46.5% (Table 5.2).
- April is also well above average at 20.5% (539 of 2,631).
- May has the lowest yes rate, 6.4% (886 of 13,767). It is a third of the call list (33.4%) but 19.1% of subscriptions (Table 5.2).
- June, July, August and November are close to each other and slightly below average, between 9.0% and 10.6%.
- The strong response in March, September, October and December is not explained by contact type. Cellular customers there subscribed at 51.9%, 49.4%, 52.4% and 44.9% in March, September, December and October respectively, roughly four to five times their rates in May, July, August and November (9.7% to 11.1%). In October telephone customers also subscribed at a high rate (40.3%, Table 5.4).
- June is unusual among the summer months: cellular customers subscribed at 42.6% (820 customers), against 9.7% to 11.1% for cellular in May, July and August (Table 5.4).

*Weekday*

- The weekday makes only a small difference. Yes rates run from 10.0% on Monday to 12.1% on Thursday (Table 5.3). Monday is the lowest day, and the other four days are within about one point of the overall 11.3%.

**What this means**

How and when a customer was contacted matters far more than the day of the week. The weak result for telephone is mainly a May-to-July result, which is also when most telephone calls were made. In those months cellular customers responded better, while in the other months the two channels perform alike. The four months with exceptionally high yes rates are a small part of the call list, and what they have in common is not visible in this data. Whether moving calls between channels or months would raise subscriptions cannot be determined from this data alone.

**Limitations**

- The data shows associations, not reasons. It cannot show whether telephone calls were less effective, or whether telephone customers in May to July were a different group or were reached in a different phase of the campaign.
- The data has no year column and covers May 2008 to November 2010, so each month combines several years. It also contains no January or February, so the month results cover ten months only.
- March, September, October and December are small months (546, 570, 717 and 182 customers). The data does not explain why they responded so well; it may reflect how customers were selected or how the campaign was run in those months.
- The month results may partly reflect the economic climate at the time, which Question 9 examines.
- No telephone yes rate is shown for March, September and December, because each has fewer than 100 telephone customers.
- Contact type, month and weekday describe the last contact of the current campaign, as documented by the dataset's authors.
- Weekday was not compared with contact type or month.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.

*Yes rate by calls received*

- The more calls a customer received, the lower the yes rate. It is 13.0% for customers who received one call, 11.5% for two, 10.7% for three and 9.4% for four. It falls to 6.3% for 6 to 10 calls, 3.7% for 11 to 20 calls and 0.8% for 21 to 30 calls (Table 6.1).
- None of the 33 customers who received 31 or more calls subscribed. This group is too small to interpret on its own.
- Customers who received six or more calls are 8.2% of the call list but received 30.6% of all calls and produced 4.0% of subscriptions, a combined yes rate of 5.5% (Table 6.1).

*The chance of a yes on each call*

- Early calls were far more productive than later ones. Per 100 calls, subscriptions were 5.6 at call 1, 5.1 at call 2, 4.4 at call 3, 3.3 at call 4 and 2.4 at call 5. They fall to between 1.0 and 2.2 for calls 6 to 10, 0.7 for calls 11 to 15, 0.3 for calls 16 to 20 and 0.1 for calls 21 to 30. No call numbered 31 or higher led to a subscription (Table 6.2).
- From the fourth call, each call produced fewer subscriptions than the campaign average of 4.4 per 100 calls (Table 6.2).
- Most subscriptions came from the first few calls. The first three calls produced 88.0% of subscriptions from 73.5% of all calls, and the first five produced 96.0% from 85.4% of calls. Calls beyond the fifth were 14.6% of the effort and 4.0% of the result (Tables 6.2 and 6.3).
- Differences between neighbouring call numbers from 6 to 10 are small, based on 12 to 75 subscriptions each, and should not be read as a pattern.

*What a limit on calls would have meant*

- A limit of 3 calls per customer would have saved 28,043 calls (26.5%) at the cost of 555 subscriptions (12.0%). A limit of 5 would have saved 15,425 calls (14.6%) and given up 186 subscriptions (4.0%). A limit of 10 would have saved 5,386 calls (5.1%) and given up 27 subscriptions (0.6%) (Table 6.3).
- Even the strictest limit shown saves 50.5 calls for each subscription given up, against 22.8 calls per subscription for the campaign as a whole (Table 6.3).

**What this means**

Calling effort was spread very unevenly relative to results. Almost nine in ten subscriptions came within the first three calls, and calls beyond the fifth took 14.6% of the effort for 4.0% of the subscriptions. A limit of three to five calls per customer would have saved a meaningful share of calls at a modest cost in subscriptions. Where the right limit lies depends on what a call costs and what a deposit is worth, and the data contains neither.

**Limitations**

- The data shows how results varied with the number of calls, not whether the extra calls caused the lower yes rate. Customers who subscribe are presumably not called again, so customers who received many calls are, by construction, those who kept declining. They are not a random sample of everyone who was called at call 1.
- Table 6.2 assumes that a subscription happens on a customer's last call. The dataset documentation ties the outcome to the end of the last contact, which supports this, but the data does not check it directly.
- The limits in Table 6.3 describe what happened in this campaign, not what would have happened under a limit. The data does not show whether customers would have subscribed with fewer calls, or whether more customers were available to be called with the calls saved.
- The data contains no call costs and no deposit values, so it cannot show where calling stops being worthwhile.
- Only calls made in this campaign are counted.
- Only 33 customers received 31 or more calls, so no conclusion is drawn about them separately.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.
