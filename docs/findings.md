# What the data says about who buys a term deposit

## About this report

A Portuguese bank contacted customers by phone between May 2008 and November 2010 to sell a term deposit, a savings product in which money is held for a fixed period. This report examines who subscribed and what was associated with a successful campaign.

**Data and definitions**

- The analysis covers 41,176 contact records. Twelve exact duplicates were removed from the original 41,188.
- The data has no customer ID, so one person may appear more than once. Each record is referred to as a "customer" for readability.
- **Yes rate:** the share of customers contacted who subscribed. The overall yes rate is 11.3%, and every group in this report is compared with it.
- The findings describe patterns in the data. They do not establish causes.
- Percentages are rounded, so a column may not add to exactly 100%.

---

## Question 1. How many customers subscribed, and how many calls did it take?

**Findings**

*Table 1.1. Calls and subscriptions by outcome*

| Outcome | Customers | Share of customers | Calls made | Share of calls | Average calls per customer | Median calls per customer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subscribed (yes) | 4,639 | 11.3% | 9,519 | 9.0% | 2.05 | 2 |
| Did not subscribe (no) | 36,537 | 88.7% | 96,216 | 91.0% | 2.63 | 2 |
| **Total** | **41,176** | **100%** | **105,735** | **100%** | **2.57** | **2** |

*Table 1.2. Calls per subscription*

| Campaign measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Total calls made | 105,735 |
| Customers who subscribed | 4,639 |
| Calls per subscription | 22.8 |

- 4,639 of 41,176 customers subscribed, a yes rate of 11.3%.
- The typical customer received two calls whether or not they subscribed: the median is 2 in both groups. The average is 2.05 calls for customers who subscribed and 2.63 for those who did not (Table 1.1).
- Customers who did not subscribe were 88.7% of the call list but received 91.0% of all calls.
- The campaign made 22.8 calls for every subscription (Table 1.2).

**What this means**

Calling effort was spread fairly evenly across customers, and most received about two calls. The higher average among customers who did not subscribe, combined with identical medians, suggests that a small group received many calls and mostly did not subscribe. Question 6 examines this.

**Limitations**

- The 22.8 calls per subscription is a campaign-wide ratio that includes every call made to customers who never subscribed. It is not the cost of winning one customer, because the data contains no call costs or deposit values.
- The data cannot show whether additional calls reduce the likelihood of a yes. Customers who subscribe probably receive no further calls, so the two groups are not directly comparable.
- Only calls made during this campaign are counted.

- The yes rate is highest at the two ends of the age range. Customers aged 65 and over subscribed at 47.3%, and customers under 25 at 24.0%, against 11.3% overall (Table 2.1).
- The high rate among older customers is consistent. Every five-year group from 65 upward has a yes rate between 40.7% and 52.9% (Table 2.2), so the result does not depend on one narrow age range.
- Among younger customers the signal comes from ages 20 to 24, with a yes rate of 22.4% (222 of 992). Ages 15 to 19 show 45.3%, but there are only 75 customers in that group, too few to draw a conclusion.
- Customers aged 35 to 54 are the largest part of the call list and responded below average. Their combined yes rate is 8.7% (Table 2.3).
- The yes rate changes sharply at age 60. It is 10.7% for ages 55 to 59 and 30.0% for ages 60 to 64 (Table 2.2). The number of customers also falls, from 3,036 to 530.
- Customers under 25 and 65 and over together are 4.2% of the call list but account for 12.3% of all subscriptions. Customers aged 35 to 54 are 53.9% of the list and account for 41.4% of subscriptions (Table 2.3).

**What this means**

The age groups that respond best, customers aged 60 and over and customers in their early twenties, are a small part of the call list. Most calls went to customers aged 35 to 54, who responded below average. Whether shifting calls toward the better-responding ages would pay off cannot be determined from this data alone.

**Limitations**

- The data shows an association with age, not the reason for it. Question 3 shows that retirement does not explain the change at age 60, because the same jump appears among customers who are not retired.
- Age may overlap with other factors. Question 3 tests job, and the time of year customers were contacted is tested later.
- The age groups were chosen for this analysis. A wide group can hide a change within it: the original 55 to 64 group concealed the jump at age 60.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.

*How the expected yes rate is calculated.* Age affects the yes rate, so a group with many older customers would look strong for that reason alone. For each group, we take the yes rate of customers in all other groups within each age group, and apply those rates to the group's own mix of ages. The result is the yes rate the group would have if it behaved like everyone else of the same age. The Result column compares actual and expected rates, allowing for the variation that chance alone produces in a group of that size. "Borderline" means the gap is only just larger than chance would produce.

*By job*

- Students (31.4%) and retired customers (25.3%) have the highest yes rates, against 11.3% overall (Table 3.1).
- Blue-collar customers have the lowest yes rate (6.9%), followed by services (8.1%) and entrepreneurs (8.5%). Blue-collar customers are 22.5% of the call list but 13.8% of subscriptions.
- Admin. customers are the largest source of subscriptions (29.1%), with a yes rate of 13.0%.
- Most other jobs are close to the overall rate, between 10.0% and 11.2% (Table 3.1).

*By education*

- University graduates have the highest yes rate among the large groups, at 13.7%. They are 29.5% of the call list but 36.0% of subscriptions (Table 3.2).
- The three groups with basic schooling respond below average: 10.2% for 4 years, 8.2% for 6 years and 7.8% for 9 years.
- Customers with unknown education have a yes rate of 14.5% (1,730 customers).
- Only 18 customers are recorded as illiterate, too few to interpret.

*Does age explain the job results?*

- The high yes rate among retired customers reflects age, not retirement. Among customers aged 65 and over, retired customers subscribed at 47.2% and customers in all other jobs at 47.6% (Table 3.4). Retired customers aged 45 to 54 subscribed at 7.1%, below the 8.7% of other jobs.
- The jump at age 60 appears with or without retirement. The yes rate rises from 12.3% to 31.1% among retired customers and from 10.3% to 29.2% among those not retired (Table 3.5).
- Retired customers aged 55 to 64 looked better than others in that range (17.1% against 12.4%, Table 3.4) because more of them are at the upper end: 225 of the 877 retired customers in that range are aged 60 to 64, against 305 of the 2,689 other customers (Tables 3.3 and 3.5).
- Student status carries its own signal. Students under 25 subscribed at 37.8% against 16.4% for other jobs, and students aged 25 to 34 at 27.9% against 11.7% (Table 3.4).

*Do the job and education results hold once age is allowed for?*

- Students respond at more than twice the rate expected for their ages (31.4% against 14.0%). Unemployed customers (14.2% against 10.4%) and admin. customers (13.0% against 9.9%) are also higher than expected (Table 3.6).
- Blue-collar customers (6.9% against 11.2%) and services customers (8.1% against 11.1%) are lower than expected, so age does not explain their low yes rates (Table 3.6).
- Retired customers are in line with the expected rate (25.3% against 24.7%), as are management, technician, self-employed, housemaid and unknown (Table 3.6).
- University graduates are higher than expected (13.7% against 9.7%). All three basic-schooling groups are lower than expected (Table 3.7).
- Age hid part of the education pattern. Customers with 4 years of basic schooling looked close to average (10.2%, Table 3.2) but are well below the 13.1% expected for their ages (Table 3.7).
- Customers with unknown education respond modestly above expectation (14.5% against 12.5%).

**What this means**

Student status, an admin. job and a university degree are associated with a higher yes rate than age alone would predict, while blue-collar work, services work and basic schooling are associated with a lower one. These are candidates for prioritising or deprioritising when deciding whom to call, but the data cannot show whether calling more customers in the higher-responding groups would produce more subscriptions. Retirement does not need to be treated as a separate signal. Age is the signal: customers aged 60 and over respond well whether or not they are retired.

**Limitations**

- The data shows associations, not reasons. It cannot explain why any group responds differently.
- The expected yes rates allow for age only. They do not allow for the month of contact, the contact method or the economic climate, which later questions examine.
- Job and education may overlap, for example blue-collar work and basic schooling. They were not tested together.
- Many groups were tested, so one or two could differ from expectation by chance alone. The borderline results (entrepreneur and high school) should not be relied on.
- The expected rate for retired customers rests on only 103 customers aged 65 and over in other jobs (Table 3.3), so it is the least solid comparison.
- Jobs and education levels are as recorded by the bank, and the data cannot show how accurate or current they are. Customers with an unknown job or education are kept as their own group.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.
