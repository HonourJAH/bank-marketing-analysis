# What the data says about who buys a term deposit

## About this report

A Portuguese bank phoned customers between May 2008 and November 2010 to sell a term deposit, a savings product where the money is locked away for a fixed period. This report looks at who said yes and what made the campaign work.

- We analysed 41,176 customer contact records. Twelve exact repeats were removed.
- The data has no customer ID, so one person could appear more than once. For simplicity we call each record a "customer".
- "Said yes" means the customer signed up for the term deposit.
- Most results are written as "out of every 100 customers called, X said yes". Overall, about 11 in every 100 said yes, and that is the yardstick we compare everything against.
- The results show patterns, not reasons. When a group says yes more often, the data cannot tell us why.

---

## Question 1. How many customers said yes, and how many calls did it take?

**What we found**
- Out of every 100 customers called, about 11 said yes: 4,639 out of 41,176. That is roughly 1 in 9.
- Most customers were called about twice, whether they said yes or no. Customers who said yes needed 2.05 calls on average. Customers who said no needed 2.63.
- Customers who said no were 89% of the list but received 91% of all the calls.
- Across the whole campaign, the bank made about 23 calls for every customer who said yes.

**What this means**
- Calling effort was spread fairly evenly, and a typical customer was called about twice. The higher average for customers who said no suggests that a small group was called many times and mostly said no. Question 6 looks at this.

**What we cannot say**
- The 23 calls per "yes" is not the cost of winning one customer. It includes every call made to customers who never said yes, and the data has no call costs or deposit amounts.
- We cannot tell whether extra calls make a customer less likely to say yes. Customers who say yes probably stop being called, so comparing the two groups is not a fair test.
- Only calls made in this campaign are counted.

---

## Question 2. Which age groups say yes most often?

**What we found**

| Age group | Customers called | Said yes | Out of every 100 called, how many said yes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 25 | 1,067 | 256 | 24 |
| 25 to 34 | 13,684 | 1,666 | 12 |
| 35 to 44 | 13,495 | 1,168 | 9 |
| 45 to 54 | 8,702 | 752 | 9 |
| 55 to 64 | 3,566 | 484 | 14 |
| 65 and over | 662 | 313 | 47 |
| **Everyone** | **41,176** | **4,639** | **11** |

- The oldest customers responded best: nearly half (47 in 100) of those aged 65 and over said yes. This holds for every five-year age group from 65 upward, at between 41 and 53 in 100, so it is not caused by one narrow age range.
- The youngest adults also responded well. Customers aged 20 to 24 said yes at a rate of 22 in 100 (222 out of 992). Customers aged 15 to 19 look even higher, but there are only 75 of them, which is too few to draw a conclusion.
- The biggest group, customers aged 35 to 54, responded below average, at about 9 in 100.
- The change happens at age 60. Among customers aged 55 to 59, 11 in 100 said yes (325 out of 3,036). Among those aged 60 to 64, 30 in 100 did (159 out of 530).
- Customers under 25 and over 65 together are only 4% of everyone called, but they produced 12% of all the "yes" answers. Customers aged 35 to 54 are 54% of everyone called, but produced only 41% of the "yes" answers.

**What this means**
- The bank's best responders, customers aged 60 and over and customers in their early twenties, are a small part of the call list. Most calls went to customers aged 35 to 54, who responded below average. Whether shifting calls toward the better-responding ages would pay off is a question this data cannot answer by itself.

**What we cannot say**
- We cannot say why older and younger customers respond more. Retirement is one possible reason for the jump at 60, but this is a guess until we compare age with job in the next question.
- Age may be mixed up with other things, such as job or the time of year customers were called. Later questions check this.
- We chose the age groups. A wide group can hide a change inside it: the original 55 to 64 group hid the jump at 60.
- Each record is a contact, not necessarily a different person.
